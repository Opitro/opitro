  - question: Does this replace a test at the clinic?
    answer: "Not remotely. A real audiogram measures the level in decibels at which you hear a tone, with a calibrated instrument, known headphones and a quiet room.   - question: Are headphones really necessary?
    answer: Yes, for two reasons. A phone speaker or a cheap desktop speaker simply does not produce the top end, so you would hear silence where your hearing is fine and conclude you had lost it. And without headphones you cannot test the ears separately, which is the single most useful thing this test can show.
  - question: The top end vanished early. Is that my hearing or my headphones?
    answer: It can be either. Inexpensive headphones do not reproduce 17–19 kHz themselves, and some roll off even earlier. Before drawing conclusions, repeat the test on a different pair. If the limit matches on both, it is your hearing; if it moves, it was the equipment.
  - question: One ear hears it and the other does not. What does that mean?
    answer: That is the most valuable thing this test can reveal. A small difference between ears is common, but a clear gap on one side is worth a visit to an audiologist. Causes range from simple and fixable, like earwax, to less so; sound from a browser cannot tell them apart, but it can show you the difference exists.
  - question: Why does the sweep seem to speed up and then slow down?
    answer: It does not — the ear is logarithmic. The step from 1000 to 2000 Hz sounds like one octave, and 8000 to 16,000 sounds like the same octave even though it covers eight times as many hertz. The sweep follows that same law, which is what makes it feel even.
  - question: Can this test damage my hearing?
    answer: It can, if you run it loud and often. A loud tone at a high frequency genuinely tires hearing, and it may not feel unpleasant while it does. Use a moderate volume, do not repeat it ten times in a row, and take the headphones off if your ears ring afterwards.

Put headphones on, set a moderate volume and press “Start the sweep”. The tone climbs from 8 kHz; the moment it disappears for you, press the button and the number freezes at your upper limit.

## Two different questions

**The upper limit** answers “how old is my hearing”: the top end goes before anything else with age, and it shows the age of the ear rather well.

**The frequency run** answers something else — whether there is a dip at a particular frequency and whether both ears hear alike. Start with “Check the volume”: a 1000 Hz tone plays at the level the test begins from, and you set your volume against it. Then pick an ear and run. On each of seven frequencies the tone rises from near silence — press “I hear it” the moment you catch it. The chart below shows where you needed it louder.

## What counts as normal

An adult usually loses the very top, and that is fine. By thirty 16–17 kHz remains, by fifty around 12. None of it affects speech: conversation lives between 300 and 3400 Hz. What is worth attention is a clear gap between the ears, or a dip in the middle of the range, where speech actually sits.

## What this page does not do

It does not diagnose and it does not draw an audiogram. A real test measures the decibel level at which you hear a tone, using a calibrated instrument in a quiet room. None of those conditions exist here: the browser knows neither your headphones, nor the volume, nor the noise around you. All it can do is produce a clean tone and ask whether you hear it. That is enough to spot a difference between your ears and to find your ceiling — and not enough to talk about hearing loss.
