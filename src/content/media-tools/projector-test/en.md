  - question: The corners are soft while the centre is sharp. What now?
    answer: "First make sure the projector is level and square to the screen: a horizontal tilt softens one side more than the other. Then look for a compromise on the focus ring — focus not on the centre but so that the targets in all four corners are equally acceptable. If the centre is sharp and every corner is equally soft, that is the limit of the lens, and no setting will fix it."
  - question: The circle looks like an oval. Is it broken?
    answer: No, almost always it is the wrong aspect ratio. Check that the source is set to the projector's native resolution and that no stretch-to-fill mode is switched on in its menu.   - question: There is a coloured fringe around the white lines. Is that a fault?
    answer: "That is panel misalignment — a common ailment of three-panel projectors, and usually a curable one. Look in the menu for convergence or panel alignment: there you can shift the red and blue panels by fractions of a pixel. A slight fringe at the very edges of the field is present on almost every projector, but a visible one in the centre is worth a service visit."
  - question: The black field looks grey. Is the projector poor?
    answer: "No, that is how a projector works: it does not block light, it paints with it. It has no absolute black — the only question is how much darker its black is. Judge by comparison rather than by the field itself: if the black squares on the checkerboard look noticeably lighter than the black field, light from the white squares is bouncing around the room, and dark curtains and walls are what fix it."
  - question: How do I check a projector before buying it?
    answer: Take a laptop, open this page and go through the charts in order. The white and grey fields show dust inside the optics (it looks like blurred dark discs rather than specks) and colour patches from a tired lamp. The dark grey field reveals glow along the edges and traces of an overheated panel. The grid shows whether the corners are soft. All of it with the lights off and after ten minutes of warm-up.
  - question: What are the rainbow flashes when the picture moves?
    answer: "The rainbow effect on DLP projectors with a colour wheel: they show red, green and blue one after another, and a quick movement of the eye lets the colours separate. Not everyone sees it, and it is not a fault. Three-panel LCD and LCoS projectors never do it at all."

Press a chart and it opens full screen. After that use the arrow keys or the space bar: with a projector the picture is on the wall while the laptop is usually across the room, and a presenter remote sends those very keys.

## The order to set things up

1. **Geometry.** Fit the green frame to the edges of the screen by moving the projector itself. Leave electronic keystone until later: it costs you resolution.
2. **Focus.** Turn the ring while watching the corner targets rather than the centre — all four should be equally sharp.
3. **Sharpness 1:1.** If the thin lines turn into grey mush, the source is not sending the native resolution.
4. **Convergence.** A coloured fringe on white lines is removed in the projector's menu.
5. **Fields.** White, grey, dark grey and black show dust, colour patches and stray light.

## What counts as normal here

Grey "black" is normal for any projector: it paints with light rather than blocking it. A slight coloured fringe at the very edges of the field is normal too. But a bright hotspot in the middle, blurred dark discs on white and clearly green or pink zones on grey are wear: dust in the optics, a tired lamp or tired panels.

## Judge it from the audience seat

A projector is checked from where the film is watched, not from beside the lens. And in the dark, without fail: it paints with reflected light, and one lamp left on in the room eats more contrast than any setting can give back.
