faq:
  - question: How does the retro sound actually happen?
    answer: Two things at once. First, samples are held in steps with no smoothing, which is where the crunch and the ringing artefacts come from. Second, the bit depth is coarsened to 3–5 bits, turning smooth changes in level into stairsteps. Simply lowering the sample rate only sounds muffled, never 8-bit.
  - question: What's the difference between the three styles?
    answer: NES is 8 kHz at 4 bits — the crunchiest and most recognisable. Game Boy is 9.5 kHz at 3 bits, coarser in depth and growlier as a result. Lo-fi is 11 kHz at 5 bits, gentler, closer to an old cassette than to a console.
  - question: Will vocals stay intelligible?
    answer: On lo-fi, mostly yes. On NES and especially Game Boy words start to disappear, and that's part of the effect. If the words need to land, take the gentlest style.
  - question: Does this turn a song into a chiptune track?
    answer: No, and it shouldn't claim to. The tool coarsens your audio; it doesn't rewrite it as notes for a sound chip. Real chiptune is a melody composed afresh for a specific chip, not a processed recording.
  - question: Can I undo it afterwards?
    answer: No, the effect is one-way — the discarded detail doesn't come back. Keep the original separately if you'll still need it.
  - question: Does the file get smaller?
    answer: Not necessarily. The audio is coarsened but saved at the same sample rate as the source, so the size depends only on the format you pick. To cut the weight, use [compress audio](/en/compress-audio).
