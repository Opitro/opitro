faq:
  - question: How is the key worked out?
    answer: The energy falling on each of the twelve notes is measured across the recording, and the resulting shape is compared against typical profiles for all 24 keys. It's a well-established method, but it is statistics rather than reading the score.
  - question: Why does it sometimes confuse major and minor?
    answer: Relative keys — C major and A minor, say — contain exactly the same notes. They differ only in which degrees carry the weight. On music with clear harmony that reads fine; on sparse or atonal material it doesn't. That's why a confidence reading sits beside the answer.
  - question: What does it do badly?
    answer: Purely percussive recordings, speech, atonal music and very short clips. If the confidence says weak, treat the answer as a guess rather than a fact.
  - question: Does it analyse the whole file?
    answer: Up to two minutes from the middle of the track. The key doesn't change while a song plays, and intros and fade-outs are the least representative parts, so this is both more accurate and faster.
  - question: What do I do with the answer?
    answer: Find the chords, match two tracks in one key, transpose a backing track to suit your voice. For the last one, [change pitch](/en/audio-pitch) shifts by semitones without touching the tempo.
  - question: Does it show the chords?
    answer: No, just the overall key. Chord-by-chord analysis is a fundamentally different problem and can't be answered honestly by this method.

Load a track and the tool measures it note by note, then reports the tonic, the mode, and how clear the match is.

## What it's good for

- **Working a song out by ear** — knowing the key tells you which chords to try
- **Matching two tracks** in one key for a mix
- **Transposing a backing track** to fit your range
- **Studying an arrangement** someone else wrote
